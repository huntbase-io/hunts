Industrial Protocol Abuse on Legacy Manufacturing Assets

Adversaries target legacy Windows 7 and XP systems used as HMIs to bridge into OT networks by using unencrypted management protocols like Telnet and FTP for lateral movement.

Brief

The Manufacturing Security

GapIn many industrial environments, the hardware that controls the plant floor is decades old. These systems, often running Windows XP or Windows 7, cannot be patched without risking production downtime or violating vendor support agreements. As noted in the Huntress report "7 Key Manufacturing Cybersecurity Trends for 2027" (https://www.huntress.com/blog/manufacturing-cybersecurity-trends), these aging assets remain a primary target for adversaries looking to bridge the gap between IT and OT networks.

The

HypothesisAdversaries exploit unencrypted management protocols on legacy HMIs to move laterally within industrial networks. Because these systems lack modern protections, attackers use services like Telnet and FTP to establish control and pivot into deeper OT segments without triggering standard alerts.

Phase 1: Inventorying Legacy

AssetsThe hunt begins by identifying every Windows 7 and Windows XP device in the environment. We query the device inventory to build a target list of workstations that likely serve as Human-Machine Interfaces or industrial controllers. This scoping step ensures that subsequent network queries focus only on the most vulnerable systems.

Phase 2: Detecting Inbound

ExposureOnce we have a list of legacy hosts, the hunt examines inbound network traffic. We look specifically for established connections on ports 21 (FTP), 23 (Telnet), and 80 (HTTP). While these protocols might be used for legitimate maintenance by local technicians, they expose credentials in cleartext and provide a simple entry point for an adversary.

Phase 3: Baselining Lateral

MovementThe most critical phase of the hunt involves stacking outbound connections from these legacy hosts. We baseline the destinations and frequency of Telnet and FTP traffic leaving the HMIs. If a workstation that usually only talks to a specific PLC suddenly attempts to connect to multiple internal assets, it suggests the host is being used as a jump box. We filter for rare destinations to highlight anomalous pivots.

Phase 4: Automated

TriageAn agent triages the results by correlating the OS version with the presence of inbound insecure sessions and rare outbound traffic. This reduces the noise for the analyst, providing a per-host verdict. If a host shows both insecure inbound management and rare outbound connections, it receives a high-risk rating for immediate review.

Blind

SpotsThis hunt has two primary limitations. First, it relies on port-based detection. If an adversary tunnels Telnet traffic inside another protocol (T1572), simple port queries will not see the activity. Deep Packet Inspection would be required to unmask such tunneling. Second, the hunt only identifies active sessions. Insecure services that are listening but have not been accessed during the lookback window will not appear in the results.

    reads hb_network_connectionsql

  3. Baseline outbound lateral movement

    Query · baseline

    Identify if legacy systems are being used as jump boxes to scan or connect to other internal OT assets via Telnet or FTP.

    reads hb_network_connectionsql

    What a hit looks like. A stack-count of outbound legacy protocol attempts. Rare destinations or high connection counts from a single HMI suggest malicious pivoting.

  4. Triage legacy protocol abuse

    Agent triage

    The agent weighs the OS version, the existence of inbound unencrypted sessions, and any rare outbound pivoting to determine risk.

  5. Evaluate triage results

    Decision

    Route malicious findings to containment and suspicious exposure to manual review.

  6. Isolate legacy asset

    Response action

    Immediately prevent further lateral movement from a confirmed compromised legacy host.

  7. Manual audit of legacy listeners

    Analyst task

    Verify if the detected unencrypted management services are business-critical and require compensating controls.

  8. Hunt closure and documentation

    Analyst task

    Document identified legacy assets and update the asset inventory to reflect current OS versions and service exposure.

Blind spots

  • Needs Deep Packet Inspection (DPI) or internal firewall logs. A sophisticated adversary might tunnel Telnet inside another protocol, making the connection appear benign to simple port-based queries. It would answer whether protocol tunneling (T1572) is masking the internal insecure traffic. Remediation: Enable application-layer visibility on internal segmentation firewalls.
  • Needs hb_network_listener with device_hostname column. The hunt only sees services that are actively being talked to. Insecure services waiting for a 'knock' are missed if no connection occurs in the window. It would answer which ports are listening on legacy hosts without active sessions. Remediation: Map device_hostname into the hb_network_listener surface.

# Industrial Protocol Abuse on Legacy Manufacturing Assets

Manufacturing environments often rely on legacy Windows systems (HMI/ICS) that cannot be patched and lack modern security controls. The adversary exploits these systems to bridge the gap into OT networks. This hunt identifies these soft targets and monitors for unencrypted protocol abuse. It inventories legacy OS versions, then fans out to detect inbound management sessions on insecure ports and outbound lateral movement to other internal assets. An agent triages the evidence so an analyst can settle the verdict.

## lateral-outbound-stack
<!-- Baseline outbound lateral movement -->
Identify if legacy systems are being used as jump boxes to scan or connect to other internal OT assets via Telnet or FTP.

```sqlite target=network role=baseline params=(scope_hosts=scope_hosts, lookback_days=lookback_days)
~~~yaml

  compare: first_seen
  window: '{{lookback_days}}d'
expected: A stack-count of outbound legacy protocol attempts. Rare destinations or
  high connection counts from a single HMI suggest malicious pivoting.

  by: device_hostname
  key:
  - dst_endpoint_ip
  rare_below: 3

- device_hostname
- dst_endpoint_ip
- dst_endpoint_port
- time
```
