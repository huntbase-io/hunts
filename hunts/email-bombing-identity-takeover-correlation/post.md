# Correlating Email Bombing Smokescreens with Account Takeover Activity

### Identifying the Smokescreen The researchers at WeLiveSecurity recently highlighted a tactic known as email bombing in their article, "Inbox flooded with sign-up messages? Email bombing explained" (https://www.welivesecurity.com/en/cybersecurity/inbox-flooded-signup-messages-email-bombing/). In these campaigns, an attacker floods a target's inbox with thousands of automated subscription emails to overwhelm them. We are publishing a hunt design to correlate these floods with account takeover attempts and follow-on scams. ### The Hypothesis An attacker is using an email sign-up flood as a smokescreen to hide security alerts while they perform account takeovers and install remote access tools via tech support scams. The distraction ensures the victim misses the one legitimate notification—the alert about a password change or a new MFA device—amidst hundreds of junk sign-up confirmations. ### Scoping and Initial Visibility The hunt begins on the hb_account_change surface. We identify users who have undergone sensitive modifications, specifically password resets or changes to multi-factor authentication factors. This initial scope focuses our efforts on accounts that an adversary would want to protect via a distraction campaign. Rather than scanning the entire estate, we prioritize users who already show signs of identity manipulation. ### Analyzing Floods and Authentication Risk The next phase moves into Google Workspace and identity logs. We query gmail message volumes to find users receiving a spike of emails—defined as more than 50 sign-up messages in a 24-hour period. In parallel, we examine the hb_auth_signin surface for high-risk authentication events or failed logins associated with those same users. A user appearing in both lists represents a high-priority lead where the flood is likely active. ### Detecting Evasion and Scam Execution Once a breach is suspected, the hunt looks for persistence and final execution. We query hb_cloud_api_activity for new mailbox rules or filters that automatically move or delete messages, which preserves the attacker's invisibility by burying future alerts. We also scan the hb_process_activity surface for the execution of remote management and monitoring (RMM) tools like AnyDesk, TeamViewer, or ScreenConnect. These tools are often installed during tech support scams to gain direct control of the victim's machine. ### Why This Is a Hunt This process is a hunt, not a simple detection, because it links behavioral patterns across three distinct surfaces: SaaS email, identity providers, and endpoints. A detection for a single RMM tool or a mailbox rule often yields too much noise for a SOC to manage. By correlating the smokescreen (the flood) with the payload (the account change or RMM tool), we isolate high-confidence threats that a single-surface rule would miss. ### Blind Spots This hunt cannot see every part of the attack chain. We lack visibility into corporate telephony logs, meaning the initial social engineering phone call remains hidden. Additionally, we cannot see floods that are processed by local email client rules that do not sync back to the server-side audit logs. Organizations should ensure advanced mailbox auditing is enabled to bridge this gap. ### How to Run the Hunt This design is provided as a hunt.md playbook. You can import it into Huntbase or any hunt.md-aware runtime to begin searching across your estate. The parameters allow you to adjust the volume threshold and lookback window to fit your environment's typical email traffic levels.
