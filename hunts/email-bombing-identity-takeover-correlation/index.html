Brief

Identifying the Smokescreen

The researchers at WeLiveSecurity recently highlighted a tactic known as email bombing in their article, "Inbox flooded with sign-up messages? Email bombing explained" (https://www.welivesecurity.com/en/cybersecurity/inbox-flooded-signup-messages-email-bombing/). In these campaigns, an attacker floods a target's inbox with thousands of automated subscription emails to overwhelm them. We are publishing a hunt design to correlate these floods with account takeover attempts and follow-on scams.

Scoping and Initial Visibility

The hunt begins on the hb_account_change surface. We identify users who have undergone sensitive modifications, specifically password resets or changes to multi-factor authentication factors. This initial scope focuses our efforts on accounts that an adversary would want to protect via a distraction campaign. Rather than scanning the entire estate, we prioritize users who already show signs of identity manipulation.

Analyzing Floods and Authentication Risk

The next phase moves into Google Workspace and identity logs. We query gmail message volumes to find users receiving a spike of emails—defined as more than 50 sign-up messages in a 24-hour period. In parallel, we examine the hb_auth_signin surface for high-risk authentication events or failed logins associated with those same users. A user appearing in both lists represents a high-priority lead where the flood is likely active.

Detecting Evasion and Scam Execution

Once a breach is suspected, the hunt looks for persistence and final execution. We query hb_cloud_api_activity for new mailbox rules or filters that automatically move or delete messages, which preserves the attacker's invisibility by burying future alerts. We also scan the hb_process_activity surface for the execution of remote management and monitoring (RMM) tools like AnyDesk, TeamViewer, or ScreenConnect. These tools are often installed during tech support scams to gain direct control of the victim's machine.

Why This Is a Hunt

This process is a hunt, not a simple detection, because it links behavioral patterns across three distinct surfaces: SaaS email, identity providers, and endpoints. A detection for a single RMM tool or a mailbox rule often yields too much noise for a SOC to manage. By correlating the smokescreen (the flood) with the payload (the account change or RMM tool), we isolate high-confidence threats that a single-surface rule would miss.

Blind Spots

This hunt cannot see every part of the attack chain. We lack visibility into corporate telephony logs, meaning the initial social engineering phone call remains hidden. Additionally, we cannot see floods that are processed by local email client rules that do not sync back to the server-side audit logs. Organizations should ensure advanced mailbox auditing is enabled to bridge this gap.

This design is provided as a hunt.md playbook. You can import it into Huntbase or any hunt.md-aware runtime to begin searching across your estate. The parameters allow you to adjust the volume threshold and lookback window to fit your environment's typical email traffic levels.

  2. Detect aggregate mailbox floods

    Query · baseline

    Identify potential email bombs by searching for rare volume spikes per user.

    reads googleworkspace_gmail_messagesql
    SELECT COUNT(*) as volume, date(internal_date) as received_on, user_id FROM googleworkspace_gmail_message WHERE internal_date >= datetime('now', '-{{lookback_days}} days') GROUP BY user_id, received_on HAVING volume > {{bomb_threshold}} ORDER BY volume DESC

    What a hit looks like. Users receiving an abnormal amount of mail in a single day. Silence proves absence only if all mailboxes were scanned.

# Email Bombing and Identity Takeover Correlation

This hunt identifies the coordination between a distraction-based denial-of-service (email bombing) and unauthorized identity changes. Attackers flood a victim's mailbox with automated sign-up emails to bury legitimate alerts from banks or identity providers. The hunt flows through a phased analysis of initial account scoping, mailbox floods, authentication risks, and follow-on evasion tactics like mailbox rule manipulation or unauthorized remote management software execution.
